# The six principles: checks and honest lines

A condensed version of each principle, enough to run the audit without the
individual skills installed.

## 1. The Itch (trigger)

People open an app because something outside prompts them or because a feeling
inside does. Lasting use comes from the feeling. Source: Nir Eyal, *Hooked*.

Check:
- Can the team state "When I feel ___, I open this to ___" per audience?
- Is it known when in the day or week that feeling occurs?
- Does each notification name something that happened for this person, or is
  it company talk ("We miss you")?
- Does the screen behind each notification deliver what it promised?

Honest line: no manufactured anxiety, no invented activity, no empty pings.

## 2. Lazy Thumb (ease)

Motivation cannot be controlled; effort can. Source: BJ Fogg's behaviour
model.

Check:
- What is the core action per audience?
- How many taps, fields and decisions from opening the app to completing it?
- Is its control the largest thing on the screen and within one-thumb reach?
- Does a partial effort count for something (draft saved, short session)?

Honest line: keep the friction that protects the user (payments, prices,
deletions, anything irreversible).

## 3. Paved Path (defaults)

People take the option already chosen. Source: Johnson and Goldstein (2003)
on opt-in versus opt-out consent.

Check:
- Is the first screen the core action, or a menu?
- Where does the app force a choice, and what is each control's initial value?
- Does each default come from the user's last choice, their context, or the
  most common choice?
- Are changes remembered?

Honest line: defaults serve the user. Consent, payments and paid add-ons are
never preselected.

## 4. Slot Machine (fresh reward, adapted)

Predictable rewards stop registering; varied ones hold attention. Source:
B. F. Skinner's variable-ratio schedules.

Check:
- What is the real reward users come for?
- What differs between a returning user's visits? Is "new since last time"
  shown first?
- Does the variation come from real change in the product?
- What does a day with nothing new look like?

Honest line: no fake scarcity or activity, no randomness around money, no
rewards that exist only to cause another open. For serious decisions, relevant
beats exciting.

## 5. Bottomless Pit (no dead ends, adapted)

Every stopping point invites the user to leave; most apps create them by
accident.

Check:
- List the dead ends: zero results, list end, empty first run, error, offline,
  success screen, removed content.
- Does each offer one relevant next step?
- Where is the user's job finished, and does the app give a clean ending?

Honest line: exits stay visible, nothing auto-advances uninvited, suggestions
are relevant, and a finished user is allowed to leave.

## 6. Piggy Bank (stored value)

Each use should leave something behind that makes the next use better.

Check:
- What would a user lose by uninstalling after a week, a month, six months?
- Which signals from their actions are recorded, and which change their
  experience?
- What can they build up and see (saves, preferences, history, progress,
  reputation)?
- How fast does a deposit pay back?

Honest line: record only what helps the user, tell them, let them see and
delete it. Stored value is a reason to stay, never a hostage. No guilt
mechanics.
