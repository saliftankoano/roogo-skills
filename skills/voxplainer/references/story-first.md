# Story-first editorial writing

Use this method when the explainer must be something people want to hear, not merely a sequence of product claims.

## Begin with a person, not the product

Give the protagonist a concrete desire already in motion. Show them doing, attempting, waiting, choosing, or risking something before explaining the platform. The product enters only when the audience understands what is at stake.

Build the dramatic spine:

1. **Desire:** What does this person want that the audience can feel?
2. **Pressure:** What compresses, hides, delays, or threatens that desire?
3. **Choice:** What action do they take with incomplete information?
4. **Reversal:** What becomes visible or newly possible?
5. **Resolution:** What credible human outcome answers the opening desire?

The outcome must remain truthful. Do not inflate a product-assisted step into a guaranteed life result. An interview, a fair hearing, a clearer decision, or saved time can be emotionally meaningful without becoming an unsupported promise.

## The Awa principle

The Awa story worked because it followed one legible causal chain:

> She already does the work → her experience is compressed into two lines → it risks disappearing → she accepts a real task → her method becomes observable → the employer stops guessing → she earns an interview.

The platform is the turning mechanism, not the hero. Awa remains the protagonist, and her demonstrated work causes the resolution.

Generalize this structure without copying its plot. Find the specific hidden value, the mechanism that reveals it, and the modest but meaningful door that opens.

## Write scenes as change

Every scene must end differently from how it began. Record:

| Field | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Starting state | What does the protagonist or viewer believe now? |
| Pressure | What makes the current state untenable? |
| Action | What visible act changes the situation? |
| New state | What can now be seen, known, or felt? |
| Handoff | Which object, phrase, sound, or movement carries us forward? |

If a scene communicates a description but no change, combine it with another beat or turn the information into an action.

## Use recurring objects as memory

Choose an object or phrase that changes meaning across the film. In the Awa story, the phone moves from opportunity, to task, to invitation; returning to the boutique bookends her journey. This setup-and-payoff structure makes a short film feel complete.

Possible recurring anchors include a document, key, message, tool, doorway, route, chair, sound, color, or repeated line. Do not repeat it unchanged: transform its meaning.

## Write for the ear

- Open in the middle of a lived situation, not with market-category exposition.
- Use concrete verbs and short clauses at emotional turns.
- Alternate compression with breathing room.
- Let the image carry facts already visible; narration should add meaning.
- Place the most memorable sentence after the viewer has evidence to believe it.
- Use silence or a brief music-only hold after an emotional reversal when it improves comprehension.
- End by resolving the opening desire, then let the brand sign the story rather than interrupt it.

## Story quality gates

Before production, test the script three ways:

1. **Listening only:** Is the causal chain clear and satisfying without visuals?
2. **One-sentence retell:** Can someone accurately retell the person's desire, obstacle, choice, and outcome?
3. **Product removal:** If the product name disappears, is there still a human story? If nothing remains, the script is an advertisement outline rather than a story.

Reject drafts that begin with a feature list, make the brand the protagonist, resolve through coincidence, or promise an outcome the mechanism cannot guarantee.
