# Animated-film storytelling and motion

Use this mode when an editorial explainer must feel like a short animated film rather than a sequence of designed slides.

## Build a dramatic spine

Center the story on a person, community, or clearly personified object with:

- a concrete desire the audience can feel;
- pressure or uncertainty that forces action;
- a choice made with incomplete information;
- a reversal that changes the meaning of an earlier moment;
- an emotional cost, not only an informational consequence;
- a resolution that answers the opening desire.

Facts are evidence inside that journey. Introduce each fact at the moment it changes what the protagonist or viewer believes. Prefer setup and payoff: establish an object, phrase, line, location, or color early, then transform or recover it later.

## Stage one living world

Favor spatial continuity over disconnected cards. Let the camera follow an action, object, or line into the next set; let a foreground object wipe the frame; or let the current environment physically transform into the next idea. Preserve screen direction and visual anchors unless a deliberate rupture expresses the story.

Use cinematic shot variation when it clarifies the action:

1. establish where we are and what is wanted;
2. move closer for the decision or transaction;
3. isolate a detail that changes the meaning;
4. show the reaction and emotional consequence;
5. widen or reveal the resolution.

## Make elements perform

For every visible non-anchor element, assign a lifecycle:

- **entrance:** where it comes from and why it appears now;
- **action:** what it does to advance the scene;
- **reaction:** how it responds to another element or new information;
- **exit or transformation:** how it leaves, breaks, combines, changes state, or becomes the next scene.

Do not fade in a finished tableau and leave it unchanged. Build, draw, unfold, stamp, count, pass, tear, scatter, collapse, repair, or otherwise reveal information through action. Animate typography as physical editorial material when appropriate: words can be typed, underlined, pushed, crossed out, torn, or reassembled.

All active layers should remain alive. Give backgrounds restrained motion such as parallax, light shifts, drifting texture, passing shadows, window movement, dust, or breathing scale. Give supporting objects secondary reactions. Keep deliberate anchors stable so the audience can track the scene.

## Control motion hierarchy

At any moment, prioritize:

- one primary action the viewer must follow;
- one secondary reaction that adds meaning;
- subtle ambient motion that keeps the world alive.

Do not animate everything with equal amplitude. Use overlap, anticipation, follow-through, weight, and varied easing so movement feels caused rather than automated. A hold may last for comprehension or emotion, but avoid unintended frozen frames longer than roughly one second.

## Connect motion to sound

Use narration as the timing backbone. When in scope, add restrained sound cues to important physical actions—paper, stamp, money, door, break, camera move, or reveal—and duck them below speech. Music should develop with the dramatic arc rather than loop without consequence.

## Plan and review

For each beat, record:

| Field | Question |
|---|---|
| Desire | What does the protagonist want now? |
| Change | What becomes different by the end? |
| Primary action | What must the viewer watch? |
| Supporting reaction | What responds to that action? |
| Environment | What subtle motion keeps the set alive? |
| Handoff | Which moving element or visual anchor creates the next beat? |
| Payoff | What earlier setup gains new meaning? |

Review the encoded video in motion. Stills can validate composition but cannot prove staging quality. Watch once for story without pausing, once muted for visual causality, and once listening without looking for narration and sound rhythm. Revise dead holds, simultaneous competing actions, unmotivated camera movement, abrupt resets, and elements that appear or disappear without a physical or editorial reason.
